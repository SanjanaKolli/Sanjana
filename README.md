## 📝 Latest Blog Post

> My Journey Into Data Engineering (and Why I'm Coming Back to It)

I didn't start out calling myself a data engineer. I started out solving problems — first in technical support, where I learned to sit with a broken system and a frustrated user at the same time, and figure out what was actually wrong underneath the noise.

That instinct — trace the symptom back to the root cause — turned out to be exactly what data engineering rewards.

Somewhere along the way, my career took a few turns I didn't expect. I moved into transition management, where my job was to take a process, a client relationship, or a body of institutional knowledge and move it — cleanly, without dropping anything — from one team to another. And I spent time in client relationship management, translating what non-technical stakeholders needed into something a technical team could actually build.

At the time, those felt like detours. Looking back, they were the training ground.

Here's what I mean. A pipeline doesn't just move data — it moves trust. Someone downstream is going to build a decision, a dashboard, a report on top of what you shipped. Transition management taught me how to hand things off without losing fidelity. Client relationship work taught me how to ask the right questions before I write a single line of ETL code, so I'm not just moving data — I'm moving the right data, in a shape someone can actually use.

So when I sat down again with AWS, SQL, Python, and Talend, it didn't feel like starting over. It felt like finally having the right toolkit for something I'd been circling for years.

I like the parts of this work that don't get talked about enough:


Designing a data model that will still make sense to the next person in six months
Writing a transformation step that fails loudly instead of silently
Explaining to a stakeholder why their report looked different this morning, in plain language, without condescension


That last one is the differentiator I keep coming back to. A lot of engineers can build the pipeline. Fewer can also sit across the table from the person who depends on it and explain, clearly, what's happening and why. I've spent years doing that second thing. Now I'm looking to bring it back into a dedicated data engineering seat.

If you're hiring, or just building something interesting with data, I'd love to talk.
